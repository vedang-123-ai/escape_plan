# Tornado Safety — Safe Rooms and What They Are Not

A "safe room" is a defined engineering term, not a description of the safest room you happen to have. This
distinction is the one most easily blurred in a consumer app, and blurring it would overstate how protected
a resident is.

## What a safe room is

A safe room is typically an interior room, space within a building, or an entirely separate building,
designed and constructed to protect its occupants from tornadoes or hurricanes
[cite: fema-safe-room-criteria]. An in-residence safe room is a small, specially designed ("hardened")
room, such as a bathroom or closet, designed as a place of refuge for the people who live in the house
[cite: fema-safe-room-criteria].

Safe rooms are intended to provide **near-absolute protection** against both wind forces and the impact of
wind-borne debris [cite: fema-safe-room-criteria]. FEMA defines the term: based on current knowledge of
tornadoes and hurricanes, occupants of a safe room built in accordance with FEMA guidance "will have a very
high probability of being protected from injury or death" [cite: fema-tornado].

## The numbers that separate a safe room from a house

This is the clearest quantitative contrast available for this peril:

| | Design wind speed (3-second gust) |
| --- | --- |
| Conventional construction, at its highest (coastal Atlantic and Gulf) | **140–150 mph** [cite: fema-safe-room-criteria] |
| FEMA-recommended safe rooms in those same areas | **200–250 mph** [cite: fema-safe-room-criteria] |

FEMA P-320 uses a hazard map with a maximum wind hazard speed of **250 mph** for design, and FEMA P-361
uses a map with four wind speed zones [cite: fema-safe-room-criteria]. The 200–250 mph design wind speed is
what is intended to provide near-absolute protection [cite: fema-safe-room-criteria].

**These are construction design criteria, not something a resident or this app can measure.** They are
recorded here to explain *why* a rated safe room differs from an ordinary interior room, not as a threshold
any part of the app evaluates.

## The standards, and which is which

- **FEMA P-361** is FEMA's guidance for safe room design and construction; Hazard Mitigation Assistance
  (HMA)-funded safe rooms must comply with it [cite: fema-tornado].
- **FEMA P-320** is FEMA's residential-focused companion guidance [cite: fema-tornado].
- **ICC 500** is the consensus standard for storm shelters; prefabricated residential safe rooms are
  described in terms of compliance with FEMA P-361 and ICC 500 [cite: fema-tornado]. Ready.gov names a
  storm shelter "built to ICC 500 standards" alongside a FEMA-criteria safe room
  [cite: fema-tornado-ready].

FEMA states plainly that although P-320 and P-361 present its guidance on the design and construction of
safe rooms, **FEMA does not verify or certify design calculations or products** [cite: fema-tornado]. No
part of this app should imply a FEMA endorsement of any specific product or room.

## What a basement is, honestly

An ordinary basement is the second rank in the shelter hierarchy [cite: noaa-tornado] and is substantially
better than an above-ground room, but it is **not** a rated safe room and has not been designed to the
wind speeds above. FEMA's own framing is that areas which are not designed as safe rooms do not provide
near-absolute protection, though they may limit the number of occupants injured or killed
[cite: fema-safe-room-criteria].

That sentence is the honest register for this app: an interior windowless room on the lowest floor is the
best available protection in most homes, and it is not the same thing as near-absolute protection. The
Red Cross phrasing follows the same order — find a safe room built to withstand high winds; the *next best*
protection is a small room with no windows on the lowest level [cite: redcross-tornado].
