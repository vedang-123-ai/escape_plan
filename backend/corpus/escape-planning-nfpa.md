# Home fire escape planning (NFPA guidance)

All content below is paraphrased from NFPA's home escape planning guidance [cite: nfpa-escape-planning] and USFA's home fire escape plan guidance [cite: usfa-escape-plans]. Nothing here is original advice.

## Core principles

- **Two ways out of every room.** Identify two escape routes from each room where possible — usually the door, plus a second path such as a window or a second doorway. A second route matters because the first can be blocked by smoke or flames. [cite: nfpa-escape-planning]
- **Sleep with bedroom doors closed.** A closed bedroom door slows the spread of toxic smoke, heat, and flames, significantly extending survival time during a nighttime fire ("Close Before You Doze") [cite: ulfsri-close-your-door].
- **Draw the plan.** Households (especially those with children) should draw a floor plan of the home marking two ways out of each room, including windows and doors. [cite: nfpa-escape-planning]
- **Routes must be usable by everyone.** Verify that each escape route can actually be used safely by every member of the household — check that windows open, screens can be removed, and security bars can be released from the inside. [cite: nfpa-escape-planning]
- **Keep exits clear.** Escape paths must be free of clutter, furniture, and stored items. [cite: usfa-escape-plans]

## The outside meeting place

Choose one safe meeting place a safe distance from the home — a neighbor's house, a mailbox, a tree, a light pole — where everyone gathers after escaping. This lets the household confirm quickly that everyone is out, and tell firefighters if anyone is missing. [cite: nfpa-escape-planning]

## Practice

- **Practice the plan twice a year**, making the drill as realistic as possible. [cite: nfpa-escape-planning]
- Let children master the escape plan in daytime practice before running a drill at night while they sleep. [cite: nfpa-escape-planning]
- Make arrangements in the plan for anyone in the home who has a disability (see `special-considerations.md`). [cite: nfpa-escape-planning]

## During a fire

- Get out and stay out. **Never go back inside** for people, pets, or possessions. [cite: nfpa-escape-planning]
- If there is smoke, **get low and go** — crawl beneath the smoke layer where air is cleaner. [cite: nfpa-escape-planning]
- Test doors before opening: if the door or handle is warm to the back of your hand, use your second way out. [cite: nfpa-escape-planning]
- Close doors behind you as you leave to slow the spread of fire and smoke. [cite: usfa-escape-plans]
- Call 911 (or the local emergency number) **from outside** the home. [cite: nfpa-escape-planning]
- If escape is impossible, keep doors closed, seal door cracks and vents, and signal for help at the window. [cite: nfpa-escape-planning]

## Fighting a fire: the decision rule

Getting out is the plan. An extinguisher is the narrow exception, and the USFA states it as a checklist
where **every** answer must be yes [cite: usfa-extinguishers]:

- Have I alerted others in the building that there is a fire? [cite: usfa-extinguishers]
- Has someone called the fire department? [cite: usfa-extinguishers]
- Am I physically able to use a fire extinguisher? [cite: usfa-extinguishers]
- Is the fire small and contained in a single object or to a surface, like a pan or a wastebasket? [cite: usfa-extinguishers]
- Am I safe from the fire's toxic smoke? [cite: usfa-extinguishers]
- Do I have a clear escape route? [cite: usfa-extinguishers]

**If you are unsure whether it is safe to use an extinguisher, and in all other situations: alert others,
leave the building, and call 911 from a mobile or a neighbour's phone** [cite: usfa-extinguishers].

Young children and older adults should not use fire extinguishers [cite: usfa-extinguishers]. The
operating mnemonic is PASS — pull the pin, aim, squeeze, sweep [cite: usfa-extinguishers].
