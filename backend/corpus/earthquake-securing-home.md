# Earthquake Safety — Securing the Home Before Shaking

Securing what can fall is the preparedness step with the clearest evidence behind it, and the one this app
can actually help a resident inventory.

## Why this step comes first

Several people died and thousands were injured in the 1994 Northridge earthquake because of unsecured items
such as toppling bookcases. A University of California, Los Angeles study found that **55% of the injuries
during that earthquake were caused by falling furniture or objects, and only one percent by building
damage** [cite: shakeout-guidance]. The remaining injuries were from people falling or behaving dangerously
[cite: shakeout-guidance].

That ratio is the reason securing contents is the first recommended action, ahead of structural work
[cite: shakeout-guidance].

## What to secure

- **Start by moving, not fastening.** Move furniture such as bookcases away from beds, sofas, and other
  places where people sit, sleep, or spend a lot of time. Move heavy objects to lower shelves
  [cite: shakeout-guidance]. Ready.gov gives the same instruction: store heavy and breakable objects on low
  shelves [cite: fema-earthquake].
- **Top-heavy furniture and appliances** should be secured to wall studs [cite: shakeout-guidance].
  Ready.gov names bookcases, refrigerators, water heaters, televisions, and objects that hang on walls
  [cite: fema-earthquake].
- **Televisions and computers** are secured with special straps made for them [cite: shakeout-guidance].
- **Small objects** are kept from falling with museum putty or wax [cite: shakeout-guidance].
- **Kitchen cabinets** take latches, so that doors do not swing open and empty their contents
  [cite: shakeout-guidance].
- **Storage racks** are kept from sliding or overturning with flexible nylon straps or bungee cords
  [cite: shakeout-guidance].
- **Gas lines to appliances** — water heaters, ovens, clothes dryers — should use flexible connections
  [cite: shakeout-guidance].

## Water heaters

A water heater is secured to wall studs with **two metal straps** [cite: shakeout-guidance]. An unsecured
water heater is called out by name as something to secure [cite: shakeout-guidance], and Ready.gov lists it
among the heavy items to secure [cite: fema-earthquake].

In California this is not advisory. Health & Safety Code § 19211 requires that **all new and replacement
water heaters, and all existing residential water heaters, be braced, anchored, or strapped to resist
falling or horizontal displacement due to earthquake motion** [cite: ca-hsc-19211]. The statute sets the
requirement but delegates the method: a water heater must be secured "in accordance with the California
Plumbing Code," or local modifications to it [cite: ca-hsc-19211]. It applies to standard water heaters of
not more than 120 gallons for which a preengineered strapping kit is readily available
[cite: ca-hsc-19211], and a seller of real property must certify compliance to the buyer in writing
[cite: ca-hsc-19211].

**Jurisdiction note.** § 19211 is California law. Other states may have no equivalent requirement. Treat
the two-strap method as the sourced general practice [cite: shakeout-guidance] and the legal mandate as
California-specific [cite: ca-hsc-19211].

## What this corpus does not claim

Sources reviewed on 2026-09-21 did **not** support two claims that a previous version of this corpus made,
so they have been removed rather than re-attributed:

- That standard drywall anchors are specifically inadequate for heavy furniture in an earthquake. The
  sourced instruction is the positive one — secure to **wall studs** [cite: shakeout-guidance].
- That earthquake-rated adhesive nylon straps are an established renter-friendly substitute for drilling.
  ShakeOut names flexible nylon straps and bungee cords for **storage racks** [cite: shakeout-guidance] and
  museum putty or wax for **small objects** [cite: shakeout-guidance]; neither is presented as a
  no-drilling replacement for securing tall furniture.

**What renters can do on this evidence:** the no-installation actions that *are* sourced — moving bookcases
away from beds and seating, moving heavy objects to lower shelves [cite: shakeout-guidance], storing heavy
and breakable objects low [cite: fema-earthquake], and museum putty for small objects
[cite: shakeout-guidance]. Anything requiring attachment to studs is a conversation with the landlord.
